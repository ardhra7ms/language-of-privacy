When “control” is not the same as power

Privacy marketing often treats control as a button, dashboard, or preference. Actual control is a bundle of powers across the full data lifecycle—and every power not offered remains with the provider.

Substantive agency Related concept: data sovereignty Hummel, Braun, Tretter, and Dabrock describe data sovereignty as a varied family of ideas concerned with agents retaining control over data. It is real, but not a single settled doctrine. View source

The person has effective, enforceable power to know, stop, correct, delete, contest, and leave—and protective defaults reduce the amount of work required.

Responsibilized self-management Responsibilization is a real concept De Wolf and Joye call the privacy-as-individual-duty discourse “control responsibility”: people are expected to learn and manage privacy while structural platform power remains in place. View source

The provider defines the choices, keeps the underlying decision power, and shifts the work—and often the blame—for privacy outcomes onto the person.

It is not a one-dimensional choice

“Sovereignty versus responsibilization” identifies a useful tension, but the concepts are not strict opposites. Move the sliders to see why. A product can give people real power while still imposing too much work, or offer effortless defaults while giving them almost no say. The nine powers below are a design framework, not a claim that every power is legally guaranteed everywhere. View source

POWER 01

Know that information exists

Not just visible account fields: sources, observed events, inferences, recipients, and retention.

POWER 02

Stop collection

Prevent recording before it happens—not merely hide, clear, or stop using an existing record.

POWER 03

Stop a particular use

Separate advertising, recommendations, model training, safety, research, and product measurement.

POWER 04

Stop a transfer

Act before information reaches another party and explain what happens to copies already transferred.

POWER 05

Correct an inference

Inspect and dispute predictions, scores, categories, and profiles—not only facts the person typed.

POWER 06

Delete stored copies

Clarify active systems, logs, backups, derived records, legal exceptions, recipients, and timing.

POWER 07

Change a default

A setting is meaningful only if the default is visible, reversible, durable, and not repeatedly undermined.

POWER 08

Challenge a decision

Receive an explanation, correct inputs, reach a human, appeal, and obtain a remedy when a system is wrong.

POWER 09

Leave without losing an essential service

Refusal is not sovereign when it requires losing work, healthcare, education, safety, or social participation.

The decisive test

Do not ask only, “Is there a control?” Ask: What power does it transfer, at which stage, against which provider decision, with what limit, and at what cost to the person?

Use “responsibilization” when...

  • privacy is framed mainly as the person's duty to read, choose, configure, and monitor;
  • the provider controls the menu of choices and underlying system;
  • protective outcomes depend on attention, skill, memory, and repeated labor;
  • failure is narrated as the person choosing badly rather than the system being poorly governed.

Use “sovereignty” carefully when...

  • the person has meaningful power across the data lifecycle, not merely interface preferences;
  • rights and limits are enforceable against the provider;
  • defaults reduce dependence on constant self-management;
  • the term specifies whose sovereignty is meant—individual, community, organization, Indigenous nation, or state.

References behind the model

  1. Daniel J. Solove, “Privacy Self-Management and the Consent Dilemma,” Harvard Law Review 126 (2013). Solove argues that notice, consent, and individual management are necessary but structurally incapable of carrying the full privacy burden.
  2. Ralf De Wolf and Stijn Joye, “Control Responsibility: The Discursive Construction of Privacy, Teens, and Facebook in Flemish Newspapers,” International Journal of Communication 13 (2019). The article explicitly connects privacy-as-control discourse to responsibilization.
  3. Patrik Hummel, Matthias Braun, Max Tretter, and Peter Dabrock, “Data Sovereignty: A Review,” Big Data & Society 8 (2021). The review shows that data sovereignty is real but plural and context-dependent.
  4. Regulation (EU) 2016/679, especially Articles 15–22. These distinguish access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, and protections concerning automated decisions.
